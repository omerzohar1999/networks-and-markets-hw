\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

The spread of epidemics in networks, such as diseases or computer viruses, presents critical challenges with significant societal impact \cite{newman2003structure,pastor2001epidemic}. Effective immunization strategies aim to vaccinate a select subset of nodes to minimize this spread \cite{pastor2002immunization,cohen2003efficient}, especially when resources are initially scarce, as seen during the early stages of pandemics like COVID-19.

In this work, we tackle the problem of \textit{adaptive vaccination}, focusing on selecting the optimal nodes for immunization based on the current state of infection spread, for times of scarce amounts of vaccines. We hypothesized that \textbf{PageRank}'s centrality, a well-established measure for identifying influential nodes, could effectively guide vaccination efforts. PageRank has traditionally been applied to web ranking \cite{page1999pagerank}, but we extend its use to vaccination strategy optimization.

We evaluate the performance of Random, Degree-based, PageRank-based, and NetShield algorithms \cite{tong2010vulnerability} under both the Independent Cascade (IC) and Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) models \cite{kempe2003maximizing,kermack1927contribution}. Our results on real-world networks demonstrate that the PageRank-based strategy consistently outperforms other heuristics in minimizing epidemic spread.

\section{Problem Formulation}
\label{sec:problem_formulation}

The goal of this work is to design a vaccination strategy that adapts to both the network's structure and the current state of an ongoing epidemic. Given a network $G = (V, E)$, where $V$ represents the set of nodes and $E$ represents the set of edges, let $S \subseteq V$ denote the set of infected (`sick') nodes at a particular point in time. With a limited vaccination budget of $k$, our objective is to identify a set of uninfected (`healthy') nodes, denoted by $\mathcal{H}$, to vaccinate in order to minimize future infection spread.

We define the adaptive vaccination problem as finding a set $\mathcal{H} \subseteq V \setminus \mathcal{S}$ of size $|\mathcal{H}| = k$ such that the expected number of infected nodes at the end of the epidemic, denoted as $\mathbb{E}_{G}^{S, \mathcal{H}}$, is minimized. This can be formalized as:

\vspace{-0.3cm}
\begin{equation*}
    \mathcal{H}^* = \arg \min_{\mathcal{H} \subseteq V \setminus \mathcal{S}, |\mathcal{H}| = k} \mathbb{E}_{G}^{\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{H}}.
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-0.3cm}

The challenge of this problem stems from its combinatorial nature, making it computationally intractable for large networks. Hence, we explore heuristic strategies that leverage network centrality measures to guide the vaccination process.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

Given that our problem formulation from \Cref{sec:problem_formulation} allows us to reason about the spread of diseases in human populations and computer viruses in digital networks, we provide a brief overview of the relevant concepts and models.

\subsection{Epidemic Models}\label{sec:epidemic-models}

We consider two epidemic models, each suited to different disease dynamics:

\paragraph{Susceptible-Infected-Recovered (SIR) Model \cite{kermack1927contribution}}

The SIR model is commonly used to study disease spread in networks \cite{anderson1991infectious, hethcote2000mathematics, shah2011rumors}. It models infectious diseases with varying recovery rates, and nodes transition through three states: Susceptible, Infected, and Recovered. Susceptible nodes can become infected, infected nodes can infect susceptible neighbors, and infected nodes recover with probability $\delta$, becoming immune.

\paragraph{Independent Cascade (IC) Model \cite{kempe2003maximizing}}

A variant of the SIR model, the IC model is used to simulate information diffusion in networks \cite{kempe2003maximizing}---by allowing nodes to infect neighbors only once.

% We use it to models diseases like COVID-19, where quick quarantine and recovery are common. Specifically, infected nodes infect susceptible neighbors with probability $p = 0.4$, but can only attempt infection once before becoming inactive.

\subsection{Vaccination Strategies}
Vaccination strategies in networks have been studied \cite{cohen2003efficient,holme2002attack}. Preemptive methods use centrality measures, like degree or betweenness, to select vaccination nodes \cite{kitsak2010identification}. In contrast, adaptive approaches consider the epidemic's current state for decision-making \cite{pastor2015epidemic}.

The NetShield algorithm \cite{tong2010vulnerability} selects nodes to minimize the spectral radius of the adjacency matrix, reducing vulnerability to epidemic spread. Our work differs by exploring PageRank centrality as a heuristic for adaptive vaccination.

\section{PageRank Centrality}

PageRank is a measure of node importance based on the idea that connections from important nodes contribute more to the importance of a node \cite{page1999pagerank}. The PageRank score $PR(i)$ of node $i$ is defined recursively as:

\vspace{-0.3cm}
\begin{equation*}
    PR(v) = \frac{\epsilon}{N} + (1 - \epsilon) \sum_{(v', v) \in E} \frac{PR(v')}{\text{out-degree}(v')}
\end{equation*}
\vspace{-0.3cm}

\noindent
where $\epsilon$ is the damping factor (typically $1/7$) and $N$ is the total number of nodes. We leverage PageRank to identify well-connected, central nodes for vaccination, as these are likely to influence epidemic spread.

\subsection{Data Modeling}

To apply the vaccination strategies to real-world network data, we first modeled the datasets to fit these strategies. We utilized and modified code from previous assignments related to network representation and centrality measures.

All networks are undirected graphs, where each line corresponds to an edge between two node IDs. Each edge is represented as two directed edges to capture the undirected nature.

\paragraph{Dealing With Sinks} Since PageRank's convergence relies on preserving the L1-norm of 1 for the ranks vector, we address nodes with no outgoing edges that would leak rank. We assume each sink node has an edge to itself during PageRank computation, thus preserving the graph's structure while adjusting the computation.

\paragraph{Evaluation Metric}

We evaluate each strategy based on the \textbf{average number of healthy nodes} remaining after the epidemic. A higher count of healthy nodes indicates a more effective vaccination strategy, averaged across simulations per configuration.

\section{Experimental Results, Discussion, and Conclusions}
\label{sec:experimental-results-discussion-conclusions}

Detailed figures for each network under both epidemic models are provided in the \hyperref[sec:artifacts]{Artifacts Appendix} (see Figures~\ref{fig:ic_results} and \ref{fig:sir_results}).

Our experiments demonstrate that the PageRank-based vaccination strategy significantly reduces epidemic spread compared to other heuristics across various networks and models. Additionally, we observe a notable runtime improvement over the NetShield algorithm, which requires solving an optimization problem to minimize the spectral radius.

\paragraph{Discussion}
The effectiveness of the PageRank-based strategy might stem from its ability to identify nodes that are both highly connected and central in the network. These nodes are more likely to influence a large portion of the network \cite{page1999pagerank}. Furthermore, PageRank's imitation of a random walk closely relates to disease spread in networks.

Our results indicate that PageRank centrality provides a more practical and computationally efficient heuristic.

\paragraph{Conclusions}
We have shown that PageRank centrality is an effective heuristic for node vaccination in the adaptive vaccination problem. Our experiments on real-world networks validate our hypothesis, demonstrating the potential of using PageRank for adaptive vaccination in network epidemics.

Future work may explore more complex dynamic vaccination strategies and investigate combining PageRank with other centrality measures to enhance vaccination effectiveness.
